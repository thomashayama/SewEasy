# Mannequin motion in the browser

Horizontal dragging physically turns a kinematic mannequin about the center of its body bounds. Vertical dragging changes viewing elevation about that same fixed pivot; scroll/pinch zooms. Shift-drag, right/middle-drag, two-finger drag and Shift+arrow keys pan the framing. A projection shift keeps both the orbit target and physical rotation pivot fixed. Recenter clears this shift. While paused, ordinary dragging inspects the frozen body and cloth without changing their physical state.

The mesh and contact solver share the same yaw transform. SDF/BVH queries transform into body space, normals transform back, and contact friction subtracts body surface displacement. Cloth positions and velocities remain in world space, so acceleration and reversals produce inertia. Each physics substep has its own aligned uniform slot; queued writes cannot collapse intermediate poses into one teleport. Angular speed and acceleration are bounded. Motion waits for the initial sewing stage; Reset returns both body and cloth to their initial state.

**Body grip.** Contact friction alone scales with how far the cloth penetrates in one substep, which for resting cloth is almost nothing. Garments held only by friction used to slide down after sewing (the pencil skirt's waist 4 cm, trousers 12 mm) and stayed put while the body turned inside them (after a 69° turn the tube top had turned 16°). Cloth pressed onto the body by its own tension is now represented by a grip: a contact pressure of 200 m/s² (the `grip` setting), times the fabric's friction coefficient, added to the push. Cloth within 2 mm of the contact shell grips without being pushed. The grip fades in with gravity after sewing, so panels still slide into place while they are sewn, and it is shared across the extra collision passes of waistbands and button plackets, so every garment gets the same grip. A weak grip is worse than none: at 40 m/s² a turning body dragged skirts through kinetic slip and gravity pulled them 28 mm down. With 200, after turning ±69° and back to the front, the tube top, pencil skirt, trousers and shirts end within 1° of the body; the circle skirt's heavy hem still twists it about 4°.

Turning uses a critically damped follower, capped at 6 rad/s and 120 rad/s². A requested quarter turn reaches within 0.02 radians of its target in 0.4 seconds. The studio passes elapsed wall time to the solver; both cloth and body advance on that clock. Pausing, hiding, scene replacement and resetting discard inactive time. Long stalls are capped at 1/30 second of work.

The solver increases substeps at lower frame rates to preserve its small step size (12 at 60 Hz, 24 at 30 Hz with default settings). Large steps otherwise weaken fitted waistbands. Ordered dispatches share one compute pass per frame to reduce CPU/driver overhead.

Validation: 15 Node tests cover fixed pivots, screen-space pan, mouse/touch/keyboard input, pause inspection, turn response, elapsed time, bounded work, distinct collider poses, controller lifecycle and garment placement. Run `node --test benchmarks/test_camera.mjs benchmarks/test_simulation_clock.mjs benchmarks/test_browser_drape.mjs benchmarks/test_collar_placement.mjs benchmarks/test_outfit_placement.mjs`. The live stress harness accepts `collar.html?scene=outfit-test.json&hz=30` (or `hz=60`); **Motion test** runs 300 settling frames and 600 turn/reversal/settling frames. **Save geometry** archives the report and final positions for seam and waistband checks.

The September 13 responsive-motion runs passed all 14 GPU fixtures and finite-position checks. The dress shirt at 60 Hz averaged 7.28 ms/frame; the shirt-and-shorts outfit at 30 Hz averaged 19.40 ms/frame, including twice as many substeps. Final collar junction gaps were zero in both captures. The outfit waistband remained at 0.973–1.041 m after 30 simulated seconds. These are local browser measurements, not performance guarantees. Reports: [`2026-09-13-responsive-motion.json`](../benchmarks/webgpu/results/2026-09-13-responsive-motion.json).

This is an upright turntable interaction, not skeletal walking or arm articulation. No backend GPU is used.
